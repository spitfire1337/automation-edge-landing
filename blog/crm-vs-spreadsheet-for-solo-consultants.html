CRM vs. Spreadsheet for Solo Consultants: What Actually Works

Every solo consultant hits the same fork in the road: keep tracking clients in a spreadsheet you already understand, or invest time setting up a "real" CRM you might abandon in a month. There's no universally right answer — but there is a right answer for your specific practice, and this guide will help you find it.

By The Automation Edge Team

If you're a solo consultant, you've probably had this exact debate with yourself at 11pm while chasing down a client's last invoice status. A spreadsheet feels free and familiar, but it doesn't remind you to follow up. A CRM promises structure, but half of them are built for sales teams closing enterprise deals, not one person juggling eight retainer clients and three warm leads. Below, we break down when a spreadsheet is genuinely the better tool, when it's time to graduate to a CRM, which free-tier CRMs are worth trying first, and how automation can quietly cover the gap that either option leaves open.

The Case for Sticking With a Spreadsheet

A spreadsheet-based client tracker is not a lesser tool — for many solo consultants, it's the correct tool, at least at first. If you have fewer than ten active clients, a well-built Google Sheet or Excel file with columns for client name, contract value, last contact date, next action, and status can do 90% of what a CRM does, with zero onboarding curve and zero monthly cost. You already know how to use it, you can share a read-only view with a bookkeeper or assistant in seconds, and you can customize the columns to match exactly how your practice actually works instead of adapting your workflow to someone else's software.

The failure mode of spreadsheet CRMs isn't the spreadsheet itself — it's that nothing forces you to open it. Unlike a CRM with a dashboard and reminders, a spreadsheet sits quietly in a folder until you remember it exists. If you're disciplined about a weekly five-minute review, it works beautifully. If you're not, client follow-ups start slipping through, and that's usually the moment consultants start googling "best CRM for solo consultants" in frustration.

Signs You've Outgrown the Spreadsheet

  • You've lost track of a client conversation. If you've ever had to ask "wait, did I already send them the proposal?" more than once, your tracking system has a gap.
  • You're juggling more than 10–15 active relationships. Below that, a spreadsheet is plenty. Above it, the manual scanning starts costing real time every week.
  • You need reminders, not just records. Spreadsheets are excellent at storing information and terrible at nudging you to act on it.
  • You're bringing on help. The moment a VA or subcontractor needs to log client updates too, a shared spreadsheet gets messy fast without strict conventions.
  • You want automatic activity logging. A CRM that logs emails and calls automatically saves the manual data-entry step a spreadsheet always requires.

A solo consulting practice rarely needs a dedicated sales platform; it needs a reliable place to answer "who am I working with and what do they need next," and a spreadsheet, an Airtable base, or a Notion database can answer that question just as well as paid software, often better, because you control the structure completely.

Google Sheets Done Right

If you decide to stick with spreadsheets, the difference between a client tracker that works and one that gets abandoned comes down to structure, not effort. Keep it to one row per client with columns for status (active, proposal sent, follow-up needed, closed), last contact date, next action, and next action date. Add conditional formatting that highlights any row where the next action date has passed — that single visual cue does most of the work a CRM's reminder system does, for free. Resist the urge to add a dozen extra columns "just in case"; the spreadsheets consultants actually keep updating are the simple ones.

When Airtable Beats a Plain Spreadsheet

Airtable is worth the slightly steeper learning curve when your client data has real relationships — for example, one client can have multiple projects, and each project has its own invoices and deadlines. A plain spreadsheet forces you to either duplicate client info across rows or maintain several disconnected tabs. Airtable's linked records handle that naturally, and its free tier's record limits are rarely a problem for a solo practice. If you've ever built a spreadsheet with a "helper tab" just to cross-reference two other tabs, that's usually the signal it's time to move to something relational.

A Simple Decision Framework

  1. Under 10 active clients, no help hired: stay with a well-structured spreadsheet. It's faster to set up and just as effective at this scale.
  2. Want automatic email and activity tracking without manual logging: try HubSpot's free CRM.
  3. Your data has real relationships — clients with multiple linked projects or invoices: move to Airtable.
  4. You already live in Notion for notes and project tracking: build your client tracker there instead of adding a fourth tool.
  5. You're bringing on a VA or subcontractor: any shared, structured system beats a spreadsheet multiple people are editing freehand.
  6. Whatever you pick, the real risk isn't the wrong tool — it's a tracker you stop updating. Automation, not more software, is usually what fixes that.

The Real Gap: Neither Option Chases Anyone For You

Here's the part both spreadsheets and CRMs quietly leave you to handle manually: neither one actually does anything on its own. A spreadsheet won't email a client whose proposal has gone quiet for a week. A CRM will store that the proposal is "pending," but it still won't send the follow-up unless you build that automation yourself or pay for an add-on. Bringing It Together

There's no universal answer to CRM vs. spreadsheet for solo consultants — a well-structured spreadsheet is often genuinely better than a CRM for a small, steady client list, while a free-tier CRM or Airtable base earns its keep once your practice grows more complex or you bring on help. What matters far more than which tool you pick is whether your system actually gets updated and whether follow-ups happen automatically instead of relying on memory.
