AED Inspection & Compliance Scheduling Automation

Searching for AED inspection scheduling software? Here's how facilities teams, schools, gyms, and corporate campuses responsible for dozens of defibrillator cabinets can auto-track the monthly visual readiness check, the pad and battery expiration dates that differ cabinet by cabinet, manufacturer recalls, and the post-use servicing an AED needs after it saves a life — so the device nobody thinks about until the exact second they need it is never the one that's dead on arrival.

By The Automation Edge Team

The Cabinet by the Elevator Had Been Dead for Four Months

A corporate campus's safety coordinator pulls an AED from a lobby cabinet during a routine audit and the status indicator is blinking red — the battery expired four months ago. Nobody noticed, because the monthly "check the AED" task had been informally passed between front-desk staff with no log and no reminder system. The campus has eleven AEDs across four buildings, each on its own pad and battery clock, and not one of those dates lived anywhere except a sticker inside the cabinet door.

An AED that's never used generates zero warning signs that it's failing — the battery expires quietly and the cabinet looks exactly as ready as a working one, right up until someone pulls it off the wall mid-emergency and it won't power on.

What AED Readiness Actually Requires, Device by Device

AED program obligations — shaped by OSHA general duty requirements, state Good Samaritan/PAD statutes, and manufacturer schedules from Philips, ZOLL, and Physio-Control — break into several independent tracking threads per device:

  • Monthly visual inspection: status indicator, cabinet signage, and pad/battery dates checked and logged.
  • Pad expiration: electrode pads carry a 2-3 year shelf life, expiring on their own installation-tied date.
  • Battery expiration and self-test: batteries run 2-5 years and periodic self-tests should be confirmed passing, not assumed.
  • Manufacturer recalls: devices can become non-compliant overnight if a serial number range is recalled.
  • Post-use servicing: pads and battery must be replaced and the unit re-verified before returning to service.

A site with fifteen AEDs installed over several years is really managing fifteen pad clocks, fifteen battery clocks, and a constant recall-monitoring obligation — and any one thread failing silently leaves a device non-functional exactly when needed.

Why a Sticker on the Cabinet Door Isn't a Tracking System

  • Staggered install dates create a dozen different clocks with no single date anyone can simply remember.
  • Staff turnover erases institutional memory the moment the person who "always checked it" leaves.
  • A dead device gives no ambient warning — the cabinet looks identical whether the unit works or not.
  • Recalls arrive on the manufacturer's schedule, and nobody is cross-referencing serial numbers unless it's automated.
  • Post-use restocking competes with incident chaos and can slip for weeks while a cabinet sits empty.
  • Multi-site programs have no portfolio view of which devices are overdue without physically visiting every cabinet.

Manual vs. Automated AED Compliance Tracking

  • Monthly checks: Manual — hoping whoever's at the desk remembers. Automated — tasks auto-generated and assigned with escalation.
  • Pad/battery expiration: Manual — a sticker nobody cross-references. Automated — every date surfaced on a reorder worklist weeks ahead.
  • Recall monitoring: Manual — nobody checking unless prompted by news. Automated — fleet serials checked on a recurring basis with instant alerts.
  • Post-use restock: Manual — depends on someone noticing an empty cabinet. Automated — logging a use instantly triggers a replacement order.
  • Audit proof: Manual — visiting every cabinet before an inspection. Automated — an exportable readiness log by device and site, ready on demand.

How Automated AED Scheduling Actually Works

  • Device-level database: every AED logged with location, model, serial number, and install date.
  • Independent recurring schedules: each device's monthly check and expiration reminders run from its own install date.
  • Digital checklists: every monthly task auto-attaches the manufacturer's readiness checklist.
  • Automated recall cross-checks: serial numbers are periodically matched against active advisories.
  • Post-use restock triggers: logging a use instantly creates a replacement work order and flags the cabinet not-ready.
  • Portfolio-wide reporting: checks, expirations, recalls, and restocks roll up into an exportable readiness report.

Five Steps to Automate Your AED Program

1. Build a device-level inventory — location, model, serial number, and install date for every unit, not a general "we have AEDs" entry.

2. Separate the monthly check from replacement clocks — run the quick visual check on its own short cycle apart from multi-year pad and battery deadlines.

3. Digitize the readiness checklist — a structured digital form captures status, dates, and condition the moment a check is finished.

4. Automate recall monitoring — a recurring job cross-references every serial number against current manufacturer advisories.

5. Trigger instant restock on use — logging a real-world use immediately generates a replacement order and marks the cabinet not-ready until resupplied.

Frequently Asked Questions

How often should an AED be visually inspected?

Most guidance calls for at least a monthly check confirming the status indicator, pad and battery dates, and cabinet condition — logged per device rather than one blanket note for the whole site.

How long do AED pads and batteries last?

Pads typically run 2-3 years and batteries 2-5 years, both tied to when that specific unit was installed or serviced — which is why devices installed a year apart rarely expire together.

What happens after an AED is used on a patient?

The pads and battery should be replaced and the unit re-verified as ready before it returns to service, ideally triggered immediately rather than whenever someone next notices the empty cabinet.

Do I need specialized software to automate this?

Not necessarily. A spreadsheet or CRM-based device inventory, a ticketing tool for restock tasks, and a no-code platform like Zapier or Make can handle device-level schedules, checklists, recall alerts, and reporting without custom development.

Practical Takeaways

AED programs rarely fail because staff can't glance at a status light — they fail because a fleet installed at different times was never going to stay organized on a sticker inside a cabinet door. Programs that actually work the one time it matters track every device's expirations individually, monitor recalls continuously, and never let a post-use cabinet sit empty waiting for someone to notice.
